Ruminations and remnants / the case for intention

I joined Toastmasters and this was my second speech. The project is called "Writing a Speech With a Purpose". When I thought about what I most want to say to people, the importance of intentionality stood out the most.

The Case for Intention

Opening

When you brush your teeth, who puts water on their brush before they put toothpaste on it? And who puts toothpaste on it with no water? And toothpaste then water? And water, toothpaste, water, anyone?

Intro

How you brush your teeth has probably become an automatic habit, to the point that you can do it on “autopilot.” Autopilot is a convenient cognitive shortcut that saves us energy when doing redundant or mundane things. But when we let autopilot take over parts of our lives that deserve our attention, it puts us at risk to dying…with the most common regret heard by hospice workers: “I wish I’d had the courage to live a life true to myself, not the life others expected of me.” The antidote to this regret is intention. I’m here today to make a case for intention in a modern society that is constantly threatening it with respect to our cognition, our competence, and our connection with others.

Input

To live intentionally, we have to start with where all actions start: the mind. They say “you are what you eat,” but I’d expand that to: you are what you consume. We live in a world where companies spend billions to get us to buy, watch, eat, and even think the way they want us to. This is on top of the societal pressures which predate today’s attention economy. All of this threatens intentionality as what we watch, read, or hear influences what we believe, how we behave, and ultimately, who we become. If we care about who we are as people, we should care about the inputs we allow into our minds.

Of course, it’s easier said than done. In an algorithmically-driven digital age, even our emotions and eyeball movements are being tracked and sold. With 95% of Americans online and 90% of online ads using personal data to target us, free will can start to feel like an illusion. But we’re not powerless. What we do have is awareness. We are aware of what’s happening and what they’re tracking to influence us. To consume intentionally, we can use these same variables that algorithms use to build a framework for how we engage with content. We can question what we’re seeing and why we’re drawn to it. We can pause and label our emotional triggers instead of reacting reflexively. We can consume with curiosity instead of compulsion. We can make decisions more carefully, even slowly—an antithetical approach in today’s fast-paced world. Yes, intentionality takes more time and energy. But, it gives us something invaluable back: agency, clarity, and alignment with who we actually want to become.

Output

Just as consuming with intention feels better, creating with intention doesn’t just feel better, it performs better. And by “creating,” I don’t just mean art or writing. I mean anything you produce: your speech, your movements, the way you think, the way you solve problems. Every action—whether it's a sentence, a step, or a strategy—is a kind of output.

And intention builds competence. Because when you act with intention, you're not just going through the motions. You're making choices. You're aligning what you do with what you mean. And that’s the difference between doing something and doing it well.

Intention also drives improvement. When you do something with intention, you’re able to notice your own patterns then refine and iterate on your techniques. In contrast, mindless repetition may also create competency, but for doing the wrong thing or the wrong way, potentially even creating bad habits.

Finally, intentionality fosters a sense of ownership. When creating with intent—whether it’s asking a question, throwing a ball, or cooking a meal—they all become expressions of who you are, your values and your personality. People can feel when something has thought and care behind it and when it doesn’t. That difference is what makes intentional output resonate with us more deeply. It’s why fine dining isn’t the same as a home-cooked meal. In the end, creating with intention isn’t about perfection, it’s about creating with your own purpose, learning from the process, and owning what you put out into the world.

Interput (I made this word up)

Finally, perhaps the most important area to introduce more intentionality is in how we connect with others. The Grant Study, which is the longest longitudinal study of adult development from Harvard, found that the single most important factor for overall well-being and longevity is the strength of our relationships.

In that case, when building our communities, we should be intentional about who is in them. Sociology studies say that you are the average of the people around you. Being intentional about who we spend time with is the difference between surrounding yourself with people who inspire, challenge, and support you versus people who diminish, drain, or deter your growth.

Intentionality is important not just for choosing new relationships but for strengthening our existing relationships. Meaningful connections don’t happen by accident. Intentionality means actively reaching out, showing up, and listening. It's very common to have friends and family in different timezones, making it more challenging to keep in touch than ever, and therefore, more crucial than ever. Ultimately, the quality of our lives depends not just on the connections we have, but on the intention we bring to choosing and nurturing those connections.

Conclusion

In conclusion, my case for intention is that it isn’t just a philosophical ideal, but a practical way to reclaim control over our minds, our actions, and our relationships in a world designed to keep us on autopilot. When we choose what and how we consume, create, and connect, we are aligning ourselves with our true values and purpose. Intentionality asks us to slow down, reflect, and act with awareness, even if it’s more difficult or countercultural. But in doing so, we can transform the mundane into the meaningful.

So as you go forward, I invite you to take one small step toward living more intentionally today, whether that’s questioning your defaults, moving more mindfully, or reaching out to something in your life. Because a life filled with intention leaves less room for regrets.
